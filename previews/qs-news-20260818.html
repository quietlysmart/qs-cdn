Map the task before AI touches it

A few weeks ago, I was sitting with a team at a private members’ club, looking at where their day-to-day work actually gets stuck. They weren’t asking for a clever chatbot. They wanted better use of member information, more relevant communications, a smoother new-member experience and clearer reports for the people running the club.

The temptation, in a conversation like that, is to reach for a tool. I’ve learned to resist it. Before you automate anything, you need to see the work as it happens.

Start with the handoff

We mapped the chain in plain language: member behaviour is gathered, someone makes sense of it, a message is prepared, the member’s preferences are captured, and the results feed back into reporting. That sequence matters because each step has a person, a decision and a handoff. Those are the places where work gets delayed, duplicated or quietly dropped.

The mapping also changed the shape of the problem. What first looked like a broad request for AI became a staged piece of work, with the customer records and the main workflow at the centre. That is a more useful starting point than choosing an app and trying to squeeze the business into it.

Write the real sequence

Most owners already carry this map in their head. That’s why it’s easy to underestimate. You know who checks the inbox, which spreadsheet gets updated, what has to be approved and what happens when someone is away. The business may run on it, but nobody else can see it clearly.

Write it down as a short account of one real job. Start when the request arrives. End when the customer, colleague or owner has what they need. Include the awkward bits: the exception, the missing information, the approval and the last-minute change. If you leave those out, the future automation will be neat on paper and wrong in practice.

Only then is AI useful. It can draft the message, sort the incoming information or prepare a first report, but it should be fitting into a known process. You can decide where it may act, where it must pause and who checks the result. You can also tell whether it has saved time or simply moved the same confusion into a new screen.

The point isn’t to produce a perfect diagram. It’s to make the repeatable task visible enough that a tool can help without becoming another person to manage. This week, spend an hour mapping one task by hand before you automate a minute of it. Pick the job that happens often, write the steps in order and mark every handoff. That page is the beginning of a useful AI workflow.

In brief

Three small developments worth knowing, each with a practical test before you change anything.
Worth being careful about Check what survives when AI-written copy is published Before a client campaign goes live, run one Claude-written draft through the actual publishing route and check what survives. Anthropic says the marking has limitations, so a clean result doesn’t prove the copy was untouched. Check the published text and any supported file information at the point where it leaves your hands. Compare it with the original draft, so you know what your own route preserves.
Tools worth knowing A harder client brief can now get more thought for free For a difficult client brief or planning question, try ChatGPT’s Think button on the free plan and compare the answer with a normal response. OpenAI says text chats are unlimited, but deeper thinking isn’t needed for every everyday task. Try it on a real planning question, then compare it with a normal response. Keep the version that helps you decide more clearly, not simply the one that sounds more detailed.
Running the business One spreadsheet handoff may be ready to leave your desk Take one repetitive job, such as moving enquiry details into a project sheet, and test whether Zapier and Google Sheets can handle it. The caveat is that automation only moves the mess faster if the fields and handoff are unclear. Start with a field that has a clear owner and a clear finish. Check a few real rows after the test, including one incomplete row.

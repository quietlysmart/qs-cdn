Write your brand down before you ask AI to help

I keep seeing the same mistake with AI and creative work. People start with the tool, then spend their time trying to correct the output. The model can produce a neat layout, a fluent paragraph or a plausible image. But it does not know what your business sounds like, what your customers notice, or which shortcuts would make the work feel cheap. It fills the gap with an average answer.

That is not a prompting problem. It is a brand memory problem. If the important decisions live in your head, AI has no reliable way to make them. You get a different version of the business every time you open a new chat.

The five-minute brand test

Before I ask AI to write or design anything, I want five things written down: who the work is for, what the business believes, how it should sound, what it should never say, and a few examples of work that feels right. This is not a 40-page brand book. It is a working brief someone else could use without sitting beside you.

The examples matter most. A list of adjectives such as modern, friendly and premium gives a model very little to work with. A real example, followed by a sentence explaining why it works, is far more useful. So is a bad example with the reason it fails. The aim is not to describe your taste perfectly. It is to make your judgement visible.

The reusable brief

I then turn that material into a short brief I can reuse. It sits above the individual task, so the context does not disappear when the project changes. For a design team, that might include spacing preferences, visual references, words to avoid, audience assumptions and the point at which a concept becomes too generic. For a small business, it might be the difference between a reply that sounds like the owner and one that sounds like software.

This is the step that moves a team from ad hoc AI use to branded prompting, the second rung on my agentic-maturity ladder. It is not automation yet. It is a shared starting point. But it gives every later workflow something solid to follow.

The useful test is simple: give the same brief to two people, or two AI tools, and compare the decisions rather than the polish. If the work still feels generic, the brief is missing something. Add another example, a constraint or a clear reason. Do that a few times and you have something much more valuable than a clever prompt. You have a small piece of your business's judgement that can travel.

In brief

Three AI developments I think are worth your attention. No launch jargon. Just what changed and why you might care.
Running the business Judge AI by completed work, not how often people use it OpenAI has proposed measuring successful work, reliability and cost instead of celebrating usage. That is a better test for a small team: did the workflow improve enough to justify keeping it?
Working with knowledge Google is folding NotebookLM into its main Gemini workspace NotebookLM is becoming Gemini Notebook. The name is less interesting than the direction: source-grounded research and business context are moving closer to the everyday AI tools teams already use.
Creative work Canva can now turn AI-built websites into editable designs Canva Code 2.0 lets you import HTML, change the layout visually and collaborate in the same editor. That makes rough prototypes easier to hand around, but someone still needs to check the experience before it goes live.
